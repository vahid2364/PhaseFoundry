The consistent pattern of coherent strain contributions significantly suppressing the spinodal in regions rich in Hf and low in Ti and Nb could be explained by examining the components of the first eigenvector of the chemical Hessian. \Cref{fig:eigenvector} displays the components of this vector for a BCC alloy with composition \ch{Hf_{0.6}Nb_{0.1}Ti_{0.1}V_{0.2}} as a function of temperature from \qtyrange{600}{1000}{\kelvin}. We see that such an initial composition fluctuation would result in regions rich in Hf and low in V and vice versa. However, Hf and V have the largest atomic radius mismatch of all four components, and such a composition fluctuation could cause large coherency strains. Consequently, when an elastic contribution describing coherent strain resulting from composition fluctuations is added to the Gibbs energy at this composition, the local curvature of the Gibbs energy is adjusted and the alloy becomes stable to the previously unstable composition fluctuation.

\subsection{Thermodynamic Stiffness and Its Effect on Phase-Field Simulations}
\label{sec:stiffness}

The stability maps and eigenvalue analysis above identify \emph{which}
compositions are spinodally unstable, but they also carry a practical
consequence for phase-field simulations that is easy to overlook.
The same Gibbs energy Hessian
$\mathbf{H}$, whose negative eigenvalues signal spinodal instability, also
controls the numerical stability of the time integrator.  Specifically, in a
semi-implicit spectral Cahn-Hilliard solver the maximum allowable time step is
bounded by the \emph{largest} Hessian eigenvalue:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta t \;\leq\; \frac{C_{\rm s}\,\Delta x^{2}}{M\,H_{\max}},
    \label{eq:dt_stability}
\end{equation}
where $C_{\rm s}$ is a safety factor (here $C_{\rm s} = 0.3$), $\Delta x$ is
the grid spacing, $M$ is the atomic mobility, and $H_{\max}$ is the largest
eigenvalue of $\mathbf{H}$ at the local composition.
An element that is thermodynamically \emph{stable} at the nominal alloy
composition — meaning its diagonal Hessian component
$H_{ii} = \partial^{2}G^\alpha_m/\partial x_i^{2} > 0$ — does \emph{not}
drive decomposition, but its large positive curvature increases $H_{\max}$
and therefore forces $\Delta t$ down.  We call such elements
\emph{numerical stiffeners}: they are spectators to the physical
transformation yet they throttle the simulation's time-stepping and
dramatically reduce the physical time accessible per unit of compute time.

It is important to emphasise that this is a \emph{purely numerical}
constraint.  The thermodynamics is correct --- the system is still globally
spinodally unstable ($\lambda_{\min} < 0$) and will phase-separate if given
sufficient physical time.  The stiffeners simply make reaching that time
computationally expensive.

\subsubsection*{Physical interpretation of the Hessian roles}

Each diagonal Hessian element $H_{ii}$ characterises the local free-energy
curvature along a single-component fluctuation direction.  A \textbf{negative}
$H_{ii}$ means the free energy is concave with respect to fluctuations in
$x_i$: the system lowers its free energy by separating into regions richer and
poorer in element $i$, which is the classical spinodal driving force.  A
\textbf{large positive} $H_{ii}$ means the free energy is steeply convex: the
system strongly resists composition fluctuations in $x_i$, and any such
fluctuation incurs a large free-energy penalty.

In the Cahn-Hilliard PDE, both types of curvature enter on equal footing
through the mobility-weighted Hessian $M\,\mathbf{H}$.  The unstable modes
($H_{ii} < 0$) provide the driving force for decomposition; the stable modes
($H_{ii} > 0$) provide thermodynamic restoring forces.  However, at the
\emph{numerical} level, the stable modes impose a stringent constraint on
$\Delta t$ through Eq.~\eqref{eq:dt_stability}: the solver must take small
enough steps that the steepest uphill curvature does not cause oscillatory or
divergent behaviour.  This is analogous to a stiff ODE where fast, stable
modes force a small step size even if the slow, unstable mode of interest
evolves on a much longer timescale.

The critical insight from \Cref{tab:hessian_comparison} is that
\textbf{reducing the Hf and Ti content does not relieve the stiffness}.
Halving both additions from 5 to 2.5\,at.\% (Nb$_{50}$Hf$_{2.5}$Ti$_{2.5}$V$_{45}$)
moves these elements further from the spinodal in the binary Hf-V and Ti-V
sub-systems, which \emph{increases} their positive curvature
($H_{\rm Hf}$: $+255\,000 \to +410\,000$\,\si{\joule\per\mol};
$H_{\rm Ti}$: $+365\,000 \to +532\,000$\,\si{\joule\per\mol}).
The stable time step is essentially unchanged at $\Delta t = \SI{1.32e-3}{\second}$,
and the growth timescale $\tau$ actually \emph{increases} from 86 to
\SI{104}{\second} because the global minimum eigenvalue weakens slightly
($\lambda_{\min}$: $-14\,811 \to -12\,228$\,\si{\joule\per\mol}).
This is counter-intuitive: one might expect that diluting Hf and Ti toward
zero recovers the fast binary Nb-V decomposition.  However, it is the
\emph{absolute magnitude} of the positive curvature — not the composition
itself — that controls stiffness, and this magnitude grows as the element
moves deeper into the stable composition regime.

\subsubsection*{Implications for alloy and simulation design}

This analysis reveals a general challenge in CALPHAD-coupled phase-field
simulations of multi-component alloys.  Whether an alloy will show
spinodal decomposition in a phase-field simulation within a tractable
number of steps depends on \emph{two separate criteria} that must both be
satisfied:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Thermodynamic criterion:} the global minimum Hessian
          eigenvalue must be negative ($\lambda_{\min} < 0$), confirming
          spinodal instability.
    \item \textbf{Numerical accessibility criterion:} no individual element
          should have a large positive curvature $H_{ii} \gg 0$ that would
          suppress $\Delta t$ below the level needed to reach
          $t \gtrsim 10^{2}\,\tau$ within a practical number of steps.
          Equivalently, all elements at the nominal composition should
          ideally reside \emph{within} or close to the spinodal.
\end{enumerate}
The stability maps of \Cref{fig:stability maps} directly address criterion~1
by identifying spinodal compositions.  They are equally useful for
criterion~2: compositions deep inside the spinodal region for all components
simultaneously will minimise stiffness and maximise accessible physical time.

When stiff spectator elements cannot be avoided — for example, when
experimental alloy constraints fix the composition — practical mitigation
strategies include:
(i)~employing fully implicit or exponential-time-differencing (ETD)
integrators, which are not bound by Eq.~\eqref{eq:dt_stability} and can take
large steps even in the presence of stiff stable modes;
(ii)~operator-splitting, which handles the stiff stable modes analytically or
implicitly while integrating the unstable spinodal modes explicitly; or
(iii)~rescaling time by recognising that the physical timescale of interest is
$\tau$, so reporting microstructural evolution in units of $t/\tau$ rather
than absolute seconds makes meaningful comparisons across alloy systems
regardless of stiffness.
